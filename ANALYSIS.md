# Critical analysis: Mean versus median

This original simulation compares two location estimators using 10,000 replicates in each of nine conditions. Every distribution is symmetric with mean and median zero and variance one. The design therefore compares the same estimand across distributions while separating tail shape from overall variance. Sample sizes of 20, 100, and 500 reveal whether the qualitative conclusions persist as information increases.

## Results and practical implications

The normal condition provides a sanity check: the mean's MSE should equal 1/n exactly. Observed MSEs were 0.04976, 0.00981, and 0.00205, close to the theoretical 0.05, 0.01, and 0.002. The median-to-mean MSE ratios were 1.45, 1.57, and 1.56. Thus the median paid an efficiency cost under normality, consistent with the large-sample normal ratio pi/2. Both estimators were theoretically unbiased by symmetry; observed biases were small relative to their Monte Carlo uncertainty.

For standardized Student t3 observations, median-to-mean MSE ratios were 0.60, 0.62, and 0.61, corresponding to about 38–40% lower MSE for the median. With 5% symmetric normal contamination, the ratios were 0.28, 0.28, and 0.29: about 71–72% lower MSE. At n=100, the paired median-minus-mean MSE differences were 0.00556 for normality, -0.00383 for t3, and -0.00744 for contamination. For the finite-fourth-moment conditions, approximate paired Monte Carlo intervals exclude zero. The t3 intervals must be treated cautiously for the reason below.

The performance figure plots n × MSE so the theoretical mean benchmark is a horizontal line at one. It exposes the persistent relative efficiency differences that an unscaled plot could hide as all MSEs shrink. The diagnostic boxplots at n=20 show both the central spread and rare extreme estimates. Such extremes explain why central box widths alone cannot determine MSE. A useful finding is that increasing n reduced absolute error without eliminating the distribution-dependent ranking. In practice, the median is attractive for symmetric heavy-tail location problems, but these results do not establish a universal best estimator.

## Fairness and limitations

Both methods receive identical observations, use fixed definitions, and require no tuning. Including normal data gives the mean a favorable benchmark; including heavy tails probes robustness. Nevertheless, selecting two heavy-tail mechanisms and one normal mechanism is a design choice, not a representative weighting of real applications. Equal variance also makes the heavy-tail distributions more concentrated near zero, which helps the median; its gains should be interpreted as a property of the whole distribution, not solely outlier resistance.

The Student t3 distribution has finite variance but infinite fourth moment. Consequently, squared errors of the sample mean have infinite variance, and conventional MCSE-based intervals for its MSE or paired MSE differences lack their usual central-limit justification. Although all simulated estimates were finite and the empirical MSEs were near theory, 10,000 replicates cannot rule out influential unseen extremes. Reported t3 error bars are descriptive rather than calibrated guarantees. Confidence is higher in the exact theoretical mean benchmark and in the direction of the large finite-moment differences than in the precision suggested by heavy-tail error bars.

The study excludes skewness, dependence, missing data, asymmetric contamination, and infinite-variance distributions. Under skewness the population mean and median differ, so a direct comparison would first require choosing a common target. Only one contamination rate and scale are considered, and neither trimmed means nor robust M-estimators are included. Estimator confidence interval coverage is outside the scope. Follow-up work should vary contamination severity, add t5 as a finite-fourth-moment comparison, and evaluate trimmed means across explicitly defined targets.

## Implementation and confidence

The main implementation challenge was preserving paired comparisons and reproducible random streams while separating generation, estimation, and evaluation. Independent replicate seeds preserve results across batching changes, and saved estimates allow auditing without regenerating observations. Six automated tests cover DGP moments, method correctness and translation, metric calculations, deterministic replication and batching, the normal-theory benchmark, and invalid input. These checks and the agreement with theory support the implementation, but cannot validate unexamined scenarios or repair the heavy-tail uncertainty limitation.
